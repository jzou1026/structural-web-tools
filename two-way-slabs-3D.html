Theory & Documentation

This section documents the mechanics implemented by the application, the numerical workflow used to create and solve the finite-element model, and the technical references supporting the formulation. The analysis is a linear-elastic plate-bending model for preliminary slab assessment and design support.

Part I: Engineering Theory
1. Scope, coordinates, degrees of freedom, and sign convention

The slab middle surface lies in the global \(x\)-\(y\) plane. Each shell node has three active plate degrees of freedom: transverse displacement \(w\), rotation \(\theta_x\), and rotation \(\theta_y\). The nodal vector for an element with \(n\) nodes is:

\[\mathbf d_e=\begin{bmatrix}w_1&\theta_{x1}&\theta_{y1}&\cdots&w_n&\theta_{xn}&\theta_{yn}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\]
Equation 1

where \(\mathbf d_e\) is the element displacement vector, \(w_i\) is the transverse displacement at node \(i\), and \(\theta_{xi}\) and \(\theta_{yi}\) are nodal rotations. Q4 elements have \(n=4\) and 12 element degrees of freedom; T3 elements have \(n=3\) and 9 element degrees of freedom.

The formulation models plate bending and transverse shear. It does not include in-plane membrane degrees of freedom, drilling rotation, geometric nonlinearity, or nonlinear concrete cracking.
2. Material properties and section rigidity

Concrete is treated as homogeneous, isotropic, and linear elastic. If the user enters zero for the elastic modulus, the application calculates:

\[E_c=4500\sqrt{f'_c}\]
Equation 2

where \(E_c\) is the concrete elastic modulus in MPa and \(f'_c\) is the specified concrete compressive strength in MPa.

\[G=\frac{E_c}{2(1+\nu)}\]
Equation 3

where \(G\) is the elastic shear modulus and \(\nu\) is Poisson's ratio.

\[\mathbf D_b=\frac{E_ch^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}\begin{bmatrix}1&\nu&0\\\nu&1&0\\0&0&\frac{1-\nu}{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf D_s=\kappa_sGh\mathbf I_2,\quad \kappa_s=\frac56\]
Equation 4

where \(\mathbf D_b\) is the plate-bending constitutive matrix, \(\mathbf D_s\) is the transverse-shear constitutive matrix, \(h\) is slab thickness, \(\kappa_s\) is the shear-correction factor, and \(\mathbf I_2\) is the two-dimensional identity matrix.

3. Factored gravity loading
\[q_u=D_f\left(\gamma_c\frac{h}{1000}+q_{SDL}\right)+L_fq_{LL}\]
Equation 5

where \(q_u\) is factored pressure in kPa, \(D_f\) and \(L_f\) are dead- and live-load factors, \(\gamma_c\) is concrete unit weight in kN/m³, \(h\) is slab thickness in mm, \(q_{SDL}\) is superimposed dead load in kPa, and \(q_{LL}\) is live load in kPa. The solver converts pressure to N/mm² before forming element nodal loads.

4. Q4 isoparametric Reissner-Mindlin element

Axis-aligned four-corner slab areas are subdivided into bilinear Q4 elements. Natural coordinates \((\xi,\eta)\) range from \(-1\) to \(+1\). The interpolation functions are:

\[N_1=\tfrac14(1-\xi)(1-\eta),\quad N_2=\tfrac14(1+\xi)(1-\eta),\quad N_3=\tfrac14(1+\xi)(1+\eta),\quad N_4=\tfrac14(1-\xi)(1+\eta)\]
Equation 6

where \(N_i\) is the shape function associated with node \(i\), and \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) are natural coordinates. The same functions interpolate geometry, transverse displacement, and rotations.

\[\mathbf J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\xi,\eta)},\qquad \begin{bmatrix}N_{i,x}\\N_{i,y}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf J^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}N_{i,\xi}\\N_{i,\eta}\end{bmatrix}\]
Equation 7

where \(\mathbf J\) is the isoparametric Jacobian, \(N_{i,x}\) and \(N_{i,y}\) are global derivatives, and \(N_{i,\xi}\) and \(N_{i,\eta}\) are natural-coordinate derivatives.

5. T3 constant-strain triangular Mindlin element

Nonrectangular polygons are triangulated by ear clipping and recursively refined. For a triangle with signed double area \(2A\), the linear shape-function derivatives are constant:

\[2A=(x_2-x_1)(y_3-y_1)-(x_3-x_1)(y_2-y_1)\] \[N_{1,x}=\frac{y_2-y_3}{2A},\quad N_{2,x}=\frac{y_3-y_1}{2A},\quad N_{3,x}=\frac{y_1-y_2}{2A}\] \[N_{1,y}=\frac{x_3-x_2}{2A},\quad N_{2,y}=\frac{x_1-x_3}{2A},\quad N_{3,y}=\frac{x_2-x_1}{2A}\]
Equation 8

where \(A\) is triangle area and \((x_i,y_i)\) are node coordinates. The application evaluates the shear interpolation at the centroid using \(N_i=1/3\).

6. Bending curvatures and transverse-shear strains
\[\boldsymbol\kappa=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{x,x}\\\theta_{y,y}\\\theta_{x,y}+\theta_{y,x}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf B_b\mathbf d_e,\qquad \boldsymbol\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_x+w_{,x}\\\theta_y+w_{,y}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf B_s\mathbf d_e\]
Equation 9

where \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is the curvature vector, \(\boldsymbol\gamma\) is the transverse-shear strain vector, \(\mathbf B_b\) is the bending strain-displacement matrix, and \(\mathbf B_s\) is the shear strain-displacement matrix.

\[\mathbf M=\mathbf D_b\boldsymbol\kappa,\qquad \mathbf V=\mathbf D_s\boldsymbol\gamma\]
Equation 10

where \(\mathbf M=[M_{11},M_{22},M_{12}]^T\) contains bending and twisting resultants in N·mm/mm, and \(\mathbf V=[V_{13},V_{23}]^T\) contains transverse-shear resultants in N/mm.

7. Element stiffness, numerical integration, and load vector
\[\mathbf K_e=\int_{A_e}\mathbf B_b^T\mathbf D_b\mathbf B_b\,dA+\int_{A_e}\mathbf B_s^T\mathbf D_s\mathbf B_s\,dA\]
Equation 11

where \(\mathbf K_e\) is element stiffness and \(A_e\) is element area. Q4 bending stiffness uses 2-by-2 Gauss integration, while Q4 shear stiffness uses reduced one-point integration to reduce shear locking. T3 bending and shear terms are integrated using the constant element fields.

\[\mathbf f_e=\int_{A_e}\mathbf N_w^Tq_u\,dA\]
Equation 12

where \(\mathbf f_e\) is the consistent element load vector and \(\mathbf N_w\) contains the transverse-displacement shape functions. Uniform Q4 and T3 loads are distributed equally among the element's transverse nodal degrees of freedom.

8. Column and wall support springs
\[k_z=E_c\left(\frac{A_t}{L_t}+\frac{A_b}{L_b}\right)\]
Equation 13

where \(k_z\) is vertical translational stiffness, \(A_t\) and \(A_b\) are top and bottom support areas, and \(L_t\) and \(L_b\) are storey heights above and below the slab.

\[k_{\theta x}=\alpha E_c\left(\frac{I_{x,t}}{L_t}+\frac{I_{x,b}}{L_b}\right),\qquad k_{\theta y}=\alpha E_c\left(\frac{I_{y,t}}{L_t}+\frac{I_{y,b}}{L_b}\right)\]
Equation 14

where \(k_{\theta x}\) and \(k_{\theta y}\) are rotational spring stiffnesses, \(I_{x,t}\), \(I_{x,b}\), \(I_{y,t}\), and \(I_{y,b}\) are effective section inertias, and \(\alpha\) is 4 for a fixed far end or 3 for a pinned far end.

\[I_x=\psi\frac{HB^3}{12},\qquad I_y=\psi\frac{BH^3}{12}\]
Equation 15

where \(B\) and \(H\) are rectangular section dimensions and \(\psi\) is the entered stiffness modifier. Wall stiffness is distributed to coincident mesh nodes using each node's tributary wall length.

9. Global assembly and conjugate-gradient solution
\[\mathbf K\mathbf d=\mathbf F\]
Equation 16

where \(\mathbf K\) is the assembled global stiffness matrix, \(\mathbf d\) is the global displacement vector, and \(\mathbf F\) is the assembled load vector. Element terms and support springs are assembled by global degree-of-freedom indices.

\[\mathbf r^{(k)}=\mathbf F-\mathbf K\mathbf d^{(k)},\qquad \frac{\lVert\mathbf r^{(k)}\rVert_2}{\lVert\mathbf F\rVert_2}\le\varepsilon_{CG}\]
Equation 17

where \(\mathbf r^{(k)}\) is the residual at iteration \(k\), \(\varepsilon_{CG}\) is the entered convergence tolerance, and \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\) is the Euclidean norm. The solver uses diagonal preconditioning and blocks result generation if convergence is not achieved.

10. Result recovery and equilibrium

Element-center curvatures and shear strains are recovered from the solved nodal vector using Equation 9, and section resultants are calculated with Equation 10. The application also compares total applied load with the sum of column and wall spring reactions.

\[\Delta R=R_{columns}+R_{walls}-P_{applied},\qquad e_R=100\frac{\Delta R}{P_{applied}}\]
Equation 18

where \(\Delta R\) is the reaction imbalance, \(R_{columns}\) and \(R_{walls}\) are summed support reactions, \(P_{applied}\) is total applied load, and \(e_R\) is equilibrium error in percent.

11. Wood-Armer-style directional design moments
\[M_{1,+}=\max(0,M_{11}+|M_{12}|),\qquad M_{2,+}=\max(0,M_{22}+|M_{12}|)\] \[M_{1,-}=\max(0,-M_{11}+|M_{12}|),\qquad M_{2,-}=\max(0,-M_{22}+|M_{12}|)\]
Equation 19

where \(M_{1,+}\) and \(M_{2,+}\) are positive bending demands for bottom reinforcement, \(M_{1,-}\) and \(M_{2,-}\) are negative bending demands for top reinforcement, and \(|M_{12}|\) is the absolute twisting moment.

12. Flexural reinforcement demand
\[A_s=\frac{B-\sqrt{B^2-4AM_u}}{2A}-A_{s,existing}\] \[A=\frac{(\phi_sf_y)^2}{2\alpha_1\phi_cf'_cb},\qquad B=\phi_sf_yd,\qquad \alpha_1=0.85-0.0015f'_c\]
Equation 20

where \(A_s\) is additional reinforcement area, \(M_u\) is the factored design moment in N·mm, \(A_{s,existing}\) is existing reinforcement, \(\phi_s\) and \(\phi_c\) are resistance factors, \(f_y\) is steel yield strength, \(b=1000\) mm is the design strip width used for per-metre reinforcement, and \(d\) is effective depth.

13. Design-strip aggregation
\[M_{strip}(s)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S(s)}M_i b_i,\qquad V_{strip}(s)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S(s)}V_i b_i\]
Equation 21

where \(s\) is station along the strip, \(\mathcal S(s)\) is the set of selected elements at that station whose centroids fall inside the strip boundaries, \(M_i\) and \(V_i\) are directional element resultants, and \(b_i\) is element width transverse to the strip. The displayed design-strip lines are centerlines; calculated strip boundaries remain based on adjacent centerlines and slab edges.

14. One-way shear resistance
\[V_c=\phi_c\lambda\beta\sqrt{f'_c}\,b_wd_v,\qquad \beta=\max\left(0.05,\frac{230}{1000+d_v}\right),\qquad d_v=\max(0.9d,0.72h)\]
Equation 22

where \(V_c\) is concrete shear resistance, \(\lambda=1.0\) is the normal-density factor, \(\beta\) is the size-effect term, \(b_w\) is design-strip width, and \(d_v\) is effective shear depth. The application caps \(\sqrt{f'_c}\) at 8 MPa.

15. Punching-demand summary
\[V_f=|R_z|-q_u(c_x+d)(c_y+d)\]
Equation 23

where \(V_f\) is estimated punching shear demand, \(R_z\) is the column vertical spring reaction, \(q_u\) is factored slab pressure, and \(c_x\) and \(c_y\) are column dimensions. Rotational spring reactions provide approximate unbalanced moments about the global axes. The output is a demand summary, not a complete punching-resistance design.

Engineering limitations. The model excludes cracking and tension stiffening, creep, shrinkage, reinforcement orthotropy, membrane action, geometric nonlinearity, material nonlinearity, explicit slab-column contact, drop panels, capitals, openings, staged construction, soil-structure interaction, and dynamic effects. Mesh sensitivity, support idealization, load path, sign convention, and design-code applicability require independent engineering review.
Part II: Coding Theory
1. Input and state layer
  • Visible dynamic tables are mirrored into hidden CSV text areas. The sync() function parses those records into the central model object M.
  • Material, load, geometric, mesh, support, and solver settings are read from named HTML controls. Global storey heights are automatically applied to every column record.
  • Undo, redo, local autosave, standalone HTML working-file export, and working-file import provide state persistence.
2. Validation and geometry layer
  • Validation checks duplicate or missing IDs, invalid references, zero-area polygons, nonpositive properties, overlapping slab areas, and incomplete support or design-strip definitions.
  • Four-corner axis-aligned rectangles are classified as Q4 regions. Other valid simple polygons are triangulated by ear clipping into T3 regions.
  • T3 triangles are recursively subdivided until the longest side satisfies the requested mesh limit or the refinement-depth guard is reached. Coincident coordinates are merged using the geometric tolerance.
3. Finite-element assembly layer
  • Each shell element forms a local bending-plus-shear stiffness matrix and a consistent gravity-load vector.
  • Local degree-of-freedom indices are mapped into sparse global matrix rows stored as JavaScript Map objects.
  • Column and wall translational and rotational springs are added directly to matching global diagonal terms.
4. Numerical solution layer
  • The sparse system in Equation 16 is solved with a diagonally preconditioned conjugate-gradient iteration.
  • The relative residual in Equation 17 is tracked against the user-selected tolerance. Nonconverged solutions are rejected.
  • Applied load and recovered spring reactions are compared using Equation 18 as an equilibrium diagnostic.
5. Recovery, design, and visualization layer
  • Element-center displacement, bending, twisting, shear, and reinforcement values are recovered after solution.
  • Design strips aggregate selected directional element resultants using Equation 21. Bending-moment and shear-force diagrams are generated as scalable SVG.
  • Contour plots use element polygons with a blue-to-red colour scale. Tooltips map pointer coordinates back to model coordinates and report the nearest element result set.
  • Flexural reinforcement, one-way shear, and punching-demand tables are generated from Equations 20, 22, and 23.
6. Reporting and persistence
  • The PDF feature prints the live Results and Theory sections using vector SVG and live MathJax content. It waits for MathJax typesetting and two browser animation frames before opening the print dialog.
  • Print CSS repeats table headers, prevents rows and key sections from splitting, scales graphics to letter-size pages, and hides interactive controls.
  • The working-file feature clones the current HTML document and rewrites the startup loader with the current input state, producing a standalone timestamped HTML file.
